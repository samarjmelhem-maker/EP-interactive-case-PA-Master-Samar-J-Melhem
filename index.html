Case 1 — The Paediatric Gentamicin Incident

A multi-stage hospital scenario combining default-acceptance, decision-support failure, BCMA workaround, and the post-incident governance response.

Setting · Hospital Tier · Tertiary Themes · 6 Difficulty · Advanced

Stage 1 — The prescription

Paediatric Ward · 22:40 Saturday night admission EP system go-live · Month 4
Clinical scenario

Layla, age 4 years, weight 18 kg, is admitted via the Emergency Department to the paediatric medical ward at a Hakeem-enabled tertiary hospital in Amman with suspected pyelonephritis. Temperature 39.2 °C, urinary symptoms for 48 hours. The on-call paediatric SHO, recently rotated from adult medicine, accesses the EP system and selects gentamicin from the antibiotics dropdown.

The system pre-populates a default dose of 240 mg IV once daily. The SHO, working through five admissions, clicks Confirm. The order is signed at 22:42. No active alert fires for paediatric high-dose. The order routes to pharmacy, where it is verified by the on-call pharmacist who is simultaneously processing TPN orders for the neonatal unit.

At 23:55 the ward nurse retrieves the dose from the automated cabinet and prepares to administer it. She notices the patient looks small.

Decision points to consider
  1. What is the safe gentamicin dose for an 18-kg child? (use 5 mg/kg)
  2. How many distinct safety controls failed in this stage?
  3. Which Koppel-family error is the dropdown default-acceptance?
  4. What governance response is required within 24 hours?

Q1 · Stage 1 reasoning

CASE 1 / Q1 MCQ · 5 options

The default-acceptance pathway in this scenario is best categorised within which of the following frameworks?

A
A latent failure in the medicines database, because the adult formulary is the only one loaded.
B
A Koppel-family human-machine interface error, specifically default-dose acceptance without weight-based recalculation.
C
A workflow violation, because the SHO did not consult the BNF before prescribing.
D
A prescribing competency gap, addressed through targeted retraining of the individual SHO.
E
A pharmacovigilance signal that should be reported to JFDA as an adverse drug reaction.
Why B is correct

Koppel et al. (2005) classified 22 EP-facilitated error types into two families. The default-dose pathway with one-click acceptance and no weight-based recalculation is the canonical example of a Family B human-machine interface error. The key feature is that the system enabled the error not by missing data, but by presenting an unsafe default that required user override to be safe.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — partial truth, wrong frame. The medicines database may indeed lack paediatric defaults, but classifying this as 'latent failure of the database' obscures the more specific Koppel taxonomy expected at master's level. The error is in the UI behaviour, not the database content alone.
  • C — workflow violation. Consulting the BNF is good practice but is not the systemic root cause. The system itself should have prevented the error regardless of whether the SHO opened the BNF. Blaming the user diverts attention from system design.
  • D — prescribing competency. The individual SHO's competency is one factor, but root-cause analysis at the system level recognises that any SHO at any hospital using this UI would face the same risk. Retraining one person does not fix a defaulting system.
  • E — pharmacovigilance. An ADR is an unexpected drug reaction. A wrong dose is a medication error, reported through incident-reporting channels (and to the patient-safety committee), not via the JFDA pharmacovigilance pathway.
💡 Examiner's tip

Classify any EP failure first by Koppel family (information fragmentation vs human-machine interface). The taxonomy is the starting point, not the conclusion — but examiners want to see you have it.

CASE 1 / Q2 MCQ · 5 options

The CDS engine fired no high-dose alert despite the prescription being approximately 2.7-fold the safe dose. Which design feature would most directly address this gap?

A
Increasing the override-justification field to a mandatory free-text reason for all alerts.
B
Disabling the dropdown defaults entirely so prescribers must type the dose for every order.
C
Configuring age-banded high-dose thresholds that compute against weight-based mg/kg ceilings drawn from a paediatric formulary.
D
Adding a hard stop on all paediatric gentamicin prescriptions that requires consultant sign-off before processing.
E
Routing all paediatric prescriptions through pharmacy verification before the order is signed.
Why C is correct

The specific gap is the absence of age- and weight-banded dose validation. The CDS rule must compute the prescribed dose against a paediatric formulary's mg/kg ceiling and fire an interruptive alert when exceeded. This is a precise, targeted control that addresses the root cause without disrupting unrelated workflows.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — mandatory free text. Forces clinicians to write a justification for every override. Worsens alert fatigue and Magrabi-style free-text discrepancies. Volume of overrides goes up; signal goes down.
  • B — disable defaults. Removes a usability feature for adult prescribing where defaults are appropriate. Breaks workflows for nephrology, oncology, and routine adult care. Over-correction.
  • D — hard stop with consultant sign-off. Strom et al. demonstrated that hard stops cause clinically significant delays — the warfarin + co-trimoxazole trial was terminated for this reason. A hard stop on all paediatric gentamicin would delay treatment for children with sepsis. Too blunt.
  • E — mandatory pharmacy verification. A reasonable additional layer, but addresses the symptom (the order reaching the patient) not the root cause (the unsafe default acceptance at the prescribing point). Also operationally infeasible 24/7 in most hospitals.
💡 Examiner's tip

When asked to design CDS, choose the most specific intervention at the most upstream point in the workflow. Broader interventions (hard stops, pharmacy verification) belong as defence-in-depth, not as the primary fix.

Stage 2 — The BCMA workaround

Paediatric Ward · 23:55 Night shift, 1 nurse to 8 patients
Continued scenario

The night-shift nurse retrieves the 240 mg gentamicin from the automated cabinet. She holds the syringe and looks at the patient — clearly small. She glances at the eMAR; the dose is signed off. The patient's wristband is on the bedside table because it was uncomfortable for the child during the day. The nurse scans the loose wristband on the table, not the patient's wrist, and scans the syringe label. Both scans clear. The eMAR records administration at 23:58.

Two hours later the on-call paediatrician reviewing admissions notices the dose, calculates 240 ÷ 18 ≈ 13.3 mg/kg, and immediately escalates. The patient receives N-acetylcysteine and intensive fluid management. Renal function is monitored over the next 72 hours. There is no acute kidney injury but a transient creatinine rise that returns to baseline by day 5.

CASE 1 / Q3 MCQ · 5 options

The nurse's action of scanning the loose wristband corresponds to which Koppel BCMA workaround category, and what is the primary risk it introduces?

A
Batch scanning — risk of administering doses meant for other patients later in the round.
B
Detached wristband scanning — defeats the bedside identification check and misses bed swaps or wristband mix-ups.
C
Pre-labelled dose preparation — risks drift between the labelled pack and the dispensed contents.
D
Override of the 'ID not confirmed' alert — bypasses the patient identification entirely.
E
Verbal-confirmation scanning — two nurses verbally agree the patient identity rather than scanning the wristband.
Why B is correct

Scanning a wristband that is not on the patient's body is the textbook example of the detached wristband workaround. The BCMA system records a successful patient identification, but no actual bedside check occurred. If the wristband had been swapped (e.g., from a previous patient discharged earlier that day) or simply belonged to a different bay, the wrong patient would receive the dose with the system's blessing.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — batch scanning. Different workaround: scanning all medications for all patients at the start of the round before going to bedsides. Not what happened here.
  • C — pre-labelled doses. Refers to labelling unit-dose packets in advance without bedside verification. This case is about the wristband, not the medication.
  • D — override of ID not confirmed. The system did not generate an ID-not-confirmed alert here because the (loose) wristband scanned successfully. There was no override.
  • E — verbal confirmation. A different workaround in which two nurses verbally confirm identity instead of scanning. Here, scanning did happen, just not against the patient.
💡 Examiner's tip

The 15 BCMA workarounds in Koppel et al. share a structural feature: they all preserve the appearance of compliance while removing the safety check. When you analyse a workaround, always ask: 'what visible sign of compliance was created? what real safety check was lost?'

CASE 1 / Q4 True / False

Increasing the BCMA scan-rate KPI alone is the most effective intervention to prevent recurrence of detached-wristband workarounds.

The answer is FALSE

The detached-wristband workaround preserves a high scan rate — the wristband is being scanned. So the KPI looks fine while the safety check is bypassed. Effective prevention requires multiple elements: addressing the root cause (wristband comfort for paediatric patients, alternative IDs like temporary tattoos or RFID), workflow design (scan-at-bedside enforcement via geolocation or proximity), nurse engagement (McNulty's ownership model), and audit (random unannounced bedside observations to verify wristband-on-patient practice).

A naked scan-rate KPI rewards the workaround.

💡 Examiner's tip

Whenever you propose a KPI, ask the inverse question: 'how could a clever person hit this number while doing the wrong thing?' If the answer is easy, the KPI is poor.

Stage 3 — Governance response

Patient Safety Committee · Monday morning 72 h post-incident
Continued scenario

The Director of Pharmacy is asked to chair an urgent root-cause analysis. The Medical Director, Director of Nursing, IT lead, paediatric consultant, and patient-safety officer attend. The CEO requests, by Friday, a written incident report and a remediation plan that will be presented to the Hospital Board next week.

The CEO asks specifically: 'Can our EP system be considered safe for paediatric prescribing right now? Should we suspend it?'

CASE 1 / Q5 MCQ · 5 options

Which response best reflects the principles of post-implementation EP surveillance and governance?

A
Suspend the EP system across the paediatric service immediately and revert to paper drug charts until a full audit is complete.
B
Maintain EP, deploy a configurable hotfix that adds age- and weight-banded dose ceilings within 48 hours, audit all paediatric prescriptions of the prior 90 days, and report findings to the Board.
C
Mandate that all paediatric prescriptions be co-signed by a consultant, and require all nurses to attend retraining within one month.
D
Assert that the system is safe because the patient was not harmed, classify the incident as a near-miss, and close the case.
E
Refer the SHO to the Medical Council, suspend the on-call pharmacist pending review, and document the incident in the staff file.
Why B is correct

This option blends three governance principles taught in the lecture: (1) continuous monitoring and refinement rather than retreat, (2) system-level fix targeting the design weakness rather than blaming individuals, and (3) retrospective audit to identify whether other paediatric patients were affected. The 90-day look-back is proportionate and feasible. The Board reporting closes the governance loop.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — suspend EP, revert to paper. Reverting to paper would re-introduce illegibility errors, transcription errors, and the J-curve known weaknesses of paper. Net safety would worsen, not improve. Disproportionate response.
  • C — consultant co-sign + nurse retraining. Both interventions place the burden on people without fixing the system. Consultant co-sign creates delays in urgent prescribing; nurse retraining addresses Stage 2 only, not Stage 1. Sociotechnical thinking demands fixing the system first.
  • D — close as near-miss. Sweeping a Class A incident under the rug. The patient received a 2.7-fold overdose; transient creatinine rise is harm, even if no AKI. Failure to act would itself be a governance failure.
  • E — punish the individual staff. Just-culture principles distinguish system errors from reckless conduct. The SHO followed the workflow the system presented. The pharmacist was working under known understaffing. Punishment would damage incident reporting and morale without reducing risk.
💡 Examiner's tip

In governance scenarios, the highest-scoring answer is almost always the system-level fix combined with proportionate audit and transparent reporting. Punitive answers and full-system suspensions are usually distractors.

Case 2 — The Discharge-to-Community Transition

A Friday-afternoon hospital discharge that stalls on its way to the community. eTP, repeat dispensing, and the open-loop nature of community pharmacy create cascading patient-safety risks.

Setting · Community + Hospital Tier · Secondary Themes · 5 Difficulty · Advanced

Stage 1 — Discharge from the medical ward

Internal Medicine, Bed 14 · 16:20 Friday 5-day admission for AF + HF exacerbation EP system go-live · Year 2
Patient · Mr. Faisal Q.

Mr. Faisal Q., 71 years, is a long-standing patient at a community pharmacy in Sweileh, Amman. He was admitted to the JUH internal-medicine ward five days ago with new-onset atrial fibrillation and a heart-failure exacerbation. He is stable, optimised, and ready for discharge.

The discharge prescription, written through the hospital EP system at 16:20 Friday, includes seven items: apixaban 5 mg BID (new anticoagulant, replaces previous aspirin), bisoprolol 5 mg OD (dose-titrated), furosemide 40 mg OD (continued), ramipril 5 mg OD (dose-titrated), atorvastatin 40 mg ON (continued), digoxin 125 mcg OD (new), and a 14-day course of furosemide 80 mg OD as an interim higher dose for two weeks.

The hospital's electronic discharge summary is generated automatically, including the medication list, dose changes (annotated 'NEW' / 'STOPPED' / 'CHANGED'), and follow-up plan. The system pushes the summary into a nightly batch transfer to community pharmacies and GP surgeries via a regional clinical portal.

Mr. Faisal goes home at 17:30 Friday with a paper printout of the discharge summary and a 7-day take-home supply from the hospital pharmacy. He plans to refill at his usual community pharmacy in Sweileh on Friday afternoon next week.

📌 Decision points to consider
  1. What hand-off failure modes are latent in a Friday-afternoon batch-transmitted discharge?
  2. How does the open-loop nature of community EP differ from the closed loop the hospital just operated in?
  3. What does the new apixaban prescription require from the community pharmacist to avoid Magrabi-style discrepancy errors?
  4. What patient-facing safeguards should be built into the transition?
CASE 2 / Q1 MCQ · 5 options

A nightly batch transmission of discharge summaries from hospital to GP and community pharmacy systems is in operational use. From a sociotechnical standpoint, what is the principal latent failure mode introduced by this design?

A
A weekend gap during which discharges generated late Friday will not reach the GP until the early hours of Monday at the earliest.
B
Encryption-key rotation is not handled for batch jobs running outside business hours.
C
Batch jobs prevent the hospital pharmacy from running stock-replenishment scripts overnight.
D
SNOMED CT codes for new medicines are not synchronised with the community formulary until the next batch.
E
Patients receive paper copies that override any electronic record at the receiving system.
Why A is correct

Kirby et al. and the Southampton/Bridgend implementations demonstrated that the principal value of electronic discharge summaries is real-time transfer — reducing the GP-information delay from ~80 days (paper) to under 24 hours (electronic). A nightly batch design negates this benefit and reintroduces a process gap. Friday-afternoon discharges are exposed to a 60+ hour window during which the receiving system has no record. This is the canonical "process design failure" described in the lecture's discharge case.

Why each other option is wrong
  • B — encryption-key rotation. A real concern for any batch system, but not the principal latent failure of nightly batches in this clinical context. Question is asking for the dominant safety risk to the patient.
  • C — stock-replenishment scripts. Operational, not a clinical-safety hand-off issue. Batch windows are routinely managed in parallel.
  • D — SNOMED CT synchronisation. Plausible-sounding distractor for students who memorise terminology. The community formulary already contains the SNOMED codes for licensed medicines; new medicines are added through formulary processes, not through discharge-summary batches. Wrong layer of the architecture.
  • E — paper copy overrides electronic. Receiving systems do not "override" anything based on a paper copy — paper and electronic are independent channels. The design problem is the absence of timely electronic transfer, not paper interfering with electronic.
💡 Examiner's tip

"Latent failure mode" is a Reason-style term — it asks which dormant condition will surface as a clinical incident under the right trigger. Pick the option that names the temporal or informational gap closest to the patient.

CASE 2 / Q2 MCQ · 5 options

Mr. Faisal has been started on apixaban as a replacement for his previous aspirin. The discharge summary annotates this as 'NEW · STOPPED PREVIOUS'. Which CDS or process feature in a community EP system most directly protects against a duplication or omission error at the next refill?

A
A formulary-restriction rule that blocks aspirin from being dispensed to any patient over 70 unless co-signed.
B
Automatic medication reconciliation that compares the incoming discharge list to the patient's existing repeat record and flags additions, deletions, and substitutions for pharmacist review.
C
A SureScripts-style transmission token that confirms delivery of the new prescription only.
D
A pop-up patient-counselling reminder triggered every time apixaban is dispensed.
E
Automatic SMS to the patient confirming that aspirin should no longer be taken.
Why B is correct

Medication reconciliation at transitions is the single highest-yield community-EP safeguard. The community pharmacist must explicitly compare the discharge list to the active repeat record and resolve every difference (NEW, STOPPED, DOSE-CHANGED). The CDS layer should surface these deltas automatically, but the pharmacist's review is the safeguard. This addresses both duplication (patient continues aspirin alongside apixaban — bleeding risk) and omission (apixaban is not added because aspirin "is already there").

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — formulary block on aspirin. Aspirin has many legitimate indications in older patients (cardiac stenting, secondary prevention). A blanket block would create alert fatigue and inappropriate prescribing barriers. Wrong instrument.
  • C — transmission token. Confirms delivery, not safety. SureScripts-style tokens prevent prescription fraud and loss in transit, not therapeutic-duplication errors at the receiving end.
  • D — counselling reminder for apixaban. Useful but does not reconcile against the existing record. If the patient continues aspirin alongside apixaban, the pop-up does nothing.
  • E — patient SMS. Helpful adjunct, but relies on patient compliance and recall. Not a system-level safeguard. Patient-facing reminders are defence-in-depth, not the primary control.
💡 Examiner's tip

At any care transition, "medication reconciliation" is the highest-scoring answer when offered. It is the WHO High 5s priority, the NICE guideline expectation, and what JCI accreditation surveys for.

Stage 2 — At the community pharmacy, six days later

Sweileh community pharmacy · Friday 13:40 Patient brings paper discharge sheet Hospital take-home supply now exhausted
Stage 2 · Refill day

Friday afternoon, Mr. Faisal walks into his Sweileh community pharmacy with the paper discharge sheet. The pharmacist, Dr. Layla, opens his patient record. The new apixaban does not appear in the system — the eTP feed received only the bisoprolol, ramipril, atorvastatin, furosemide, and digoxin items. The apixaban and the 14-day high-dose furosemide were dropped during the inter-system mapping because the hospital used a hospital-specific item code that did not resolve to the community drug catalogue.

The patient asks Dr. Layla in passing: "They told me to stop the aspirin. I have been doing that, right?" Dr. Layla checks his repeat: aspirin 75 mg OD is still active, last dispensed three weeks before admission.

Dr. Layla now has 30 minutes before the pharmacy closes for Friday prayer. The hospital's medicines-information line is closed for the weekend. The patient lives a 35-minute drive away and has no transport back if he leaves empty-handed.

CASE 2 / Q3 MCQ · 5 options

Two prescription items dropped during the inter-system mapping because of code-mismatch between the hospital catalogue and the community catalogue. Which interoperability standard is most directly designed to prevent this class of failure?

A
HL7 v2 ADT messaging used to keep patient demographic data in sync.
B
A national medicines dictionary such as dm+d in the UK or RxNorm in the US, mapped to a SNOMED CT code spine and adopted by both sender and receiver.
C
LOINC codes for laboratory observations applied to medicine names.
D
GS1 barcode standards on the manufacturer packaging.
E
A Hakeem-style central EHR overlay that bypasses local catalogues entirely.
Why B is correct

This is a terminology binding failure. Two different systems each held an internal item code for the same medicine; without a shared semantic spine, the mapping cannot reliably resolve. dm+d (UK) and RxNorm (US) are the canonical national medicines dictionaries — both built on a SNOMED CT scaffold. Adoption by both sender and receiver is what prevents items from being silently dropped or substituted at the boundary.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — HL7 v2 ADT. ADT is admission-discharge-transfer demographic messaging, not medication content. HL7 is the transport layer here; the failure is at the semantic layer.
  • C — LOINC. LOINC codes laboratory observations and clinical assessments. Not for medicines. Distractor for students who pattern-match standards names.
  • D — GS1 barcodes. Operates at the physical-pack level for BCMA, not at the prescription-data layer for inter-system transfer.
  • E — Hakeem central overlay. A central national EHR removes the inter-system mapping problem only for organisations on Hakeem; a community pharmacy in private hands is generally not yet integrated. Even where central overlays exist, they themselves use a medicines dictionary internally — the answer points to the underlying mechanism.
💡 Examiner's tip

Distinguish the three layers of interoperability in any answer: transport (HL7, FHIR), terminology (SNOMED, dm+d, RxNorm, LOINC), and identity (NHS number, MRN, national ID). Code-mismatch is always a terminology problem.

CASE 2 / Q4 True / False

Statement: in a Hakeem-integrated facility, the failure described above (two items dropped during inter-system mapping) cannot occur, because Hakeem is a single instance of the EHR shared across all integrated facilities.

TRUE
FALSE
Why FALSE is correct

The statement contains a true premise (Hakeem is indeed designed as a shared EHR across integrated facilities) attached to an unsafe conclusion. Code-mismatch can still occur wherever a non-integrated entity sits in the data path: a private-sector hospital, an out-of-network community pharmacy, a private GP surgery, or an external imaging provider. The failure described above happens at the boundary between integrated and non-integrated systems — exactly where most Jordanian community pharmacies sit today. Hakeem's integration footprint reduces the problem; it does not eliminate it.

Why TRUE is wrong

Treating a national EHR as a complete solution is a common student mistake — it conflates the design ambition (one record per patient) with the empirical reality (private sector, cross-border, paper-based and parallel-electronic processes coexist). Examiners want nuance: integration is a spectrum, not a binary.

💡 Examiner's tip

For Jordan-specific questions, always name three things: (1) Hakeem's footprint (MoH facilities, RMS, KHCC since 2009), (2) the unintegrated tail (private hospitals, community pharmacies, JFDA pharmacovigilance flow), (3) the boundary risks where data crosses between them.

CASE 2 / Q5 MCQ · 5 options

Considering everything above, what is the correct first action for Dr. Layla in the next 30 minutes?

A
Dispense the five items present in the eTP feed and ask the patient to return on Monday for the apixaban once the hospital can be reached.
B
Verify the apixaban dose and the high-dose furosemide course directly from the patient's paper discharge summary, document the source of the verification, deactivate the aspirin from the active repeat list, dispense all seven items, and notify the prescriber on Monday.
C
Decline to dispense any new items not present in the eTP feed and refer the patient back to the hospital pharmacy.
D
Dispense the apixaban and continue dispensing aspirin so the patient does not run short of either.
E
Provide a 7-day emergency supply of all seven items at her own discretion and complete reconciliation the following week.
Why B is correct

B is the only option that does all four things the situation requires: (1) verifies the missing items against a primary source (paper discharge summary, signed by the hospital prescriber); (2) reconciles the active repeat record by deactivating the aspirin that the discharge explicitly stopped; (3) dispenses the medicines the patient clinically needs to start tonight; (4) documents the source of verification and notifies the prescriber. This is medication reconciliation in action, executed under time pressure with a defensible audit trail.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A — withhold apixaban until Monday. Apixaban is being newly initiated for AF in a 71-year-old. A 60-hour gap risks thromboembolic stroke. Withholding because of a system gap, when a verifying source is in the patient's hand, prioritises the system over the patient.
  • C — refer back to hospital. 35-minute drive, weekend, no transport. The pharmacist holds the necessary primary source. This is moral hazard — passing the problem along when the pharmacist has authority to resolve it.
  • D — continue aspirin alongside apixaban. Active dual antiplatelet + DOAC is a high bleeding risk. The discharge explicitly stopped aspirin. This option is the duplication error the case is testing for.
  • E — emergency supply at discretion. Emergency supply is a defined regulatory mechanism for specific circumstances and durations. Using it as a routine workaround for system failures medicalises a process problem and is professionally indefensible at scale.
💡 Examiner's tip

When a community-pharmacy scenario gives you a patient + a paper source + a system gap, the answer is almost always "verify, reconcile, dispense, document, notify" — in that order. Memorise the verbs.

Card 1 · Definition
Define EP and contrast with CPOE in one sentence.
Tap to reveal
Answer
EP covers the full hospital medicines cycle (prescribe → supply → administer → monitor) with integrated decision support and audit trail; CPOE is data capture only and may exist without decision support.
Card 2 · Bates evidence
Headline numbers from Bates et al. on EP-attributable error reduction.
Tap to reveal
Answer
54% ↓ in non-intercepted serious errors; 17% ↓ in preventable ADEs; 84% ↓ in non-intercepted potential ADEs; 81% ↓ in overall non-missed-dose errors. The 17% number is the clinically meaningful but most modest figure.
Card 3 · Koppel
What did Koppel et al. (2005) find, and what are the two error families?
Tap to reveal
Answer
22 EP-facilitated error types after 7 years of use, in two families: (A) fragmented data & poor integration; (B) problematic human-machine interfaces. EP creates new error classes that did not exist on paper.
Card 4 · J-curve
Why do error rates initially increase post-EP go-live?
Tap to reveal
Answer
Three reasons: (1) enhanced detection — electronic systems capture errors paper missed; (2) learning curve — staff adapting to new workflows; (3) design flaws — system enables new error types. Persistent rises signal design problems requiring intervention.
Card 5 · BCMA
Five rights of BCMA and the Poon evidence.
Tap to reveal
Answer
Right patient, drug, dose, route, time — verified by scanning patient wristband and medication. Poon et al. analysed 369,000 events: 74% ↓ in target adverse events; Charing Cross reduced non-definitive patient ID from 82.6% to 18.9%.
Card 6 · Workarounds
How many BCMA workarounds did Koppel identify, and what are the four root causes?
Tap to reveal
Answer
15 distinct workarounds across five hospitals. Causes: (1) technical failures; (2) time pressure; (3) lack of awareness (agency / new staff); (4) workaround culture (normalisation of deviance).
Card 7 · CDS types
Active vs Passive decision support — define and give examples.
Tap to reveal
Answer
Active: automatic interruptive alerts during workflow (allergy, interaction, dose-range checks). Passive: reference resources accessed when user seeks them (BNF, formulary, UpToDate). Active catches errors missed by clinicians but risks alert fatigue; passive provides depth but is easily ignored.
Card 8 · Warning fatigue
Huntemann finding and the structural fix.
Tap to reveal
Answer
97% of allergy alerts overridden (Huntemann et al.). Fix = graded alerts: reserve interruptive alerts for high-severity events; minor interactions presented as passive information; track override rates and retire alerts >80% override.
Card 9 · Hard stops
When are hard stops appropriate, and what is the Strom warning?
Tap to reveal
Answer
Reserve hard stops for absolute contraindications and never-events (intrathecal vincristine, daily methotrexate). Strom et al. terminated a warfarin + co-trimoxazole hard-stop trial because it caused clinically significant treatment delays. Use soft alerts requiring justification for relative contraindications.
Card 10 · Magrabi
Free-text discrepancy rate and clinical consequence.
Tap to reveal
Answer
Magrabi et al. analysed 2,914 prescriptions with free-text fields. 16.1% had internal discrepancies between structured fields and free text; 83.8% of those discrepancies led to adverse events (16.8% severe). Design principle: minimise free text; use structured fields with validation.
Card 11 · Standards stack
Name the five interoperability standards every EP system rests on.
Tap to reveal
Answer
HL7 / FHIR (transport & messaging), SNOMED CT (clinical terminology spine), dm+d / RxNorm (national medicines dictionary), LOINC (lab observations), GS1 (barcode standard for BCMA).
Card 12 · Sociotechnical
State the five sociotechnical lessons of EP in one phrase each.
Tap to reveal
Answer
(1) EP ≠ plug-and-play; (2) Design determines benefit; (3) Culture matters; (4) Integrate, don't isolate; (5) Monitor and adapt. Technology is necessary but never sufficient.

MCQ / 1Definitions

A consultant describes their hospital's new system as "EP-equivalent CPOE." Which of the following is the most accurate interpretation of this description?

A
The system has decision support, full administration tracking, and an integrated audit trail equivalent to a UK EP system.
B
The system captures prescriber order entry but may lack integrated decision support, administration recording, or both.
C
The system performs eTP between hospital and community pharmacies as its primary function.
D
The system is FDA-cleared for use in US hospitals only.
E
The system runs on closed-network architecture rather than wireless infrastructure.
Why B is correct

"CPOE" is a US term that describes data capture only. A consultant calling something "EP-equivalent CPOE" is essentially describing CPOE — and the listener should treat that as a flag that decision support and administration modules may not be present, despite the marketing language. The lecture's terminology slide warns explicitly that these terms are often used loosely; the safe interpretation is the narrower one.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. If decision support and administration tracking were present, the consultant would not need the "CPOE" qualifier. The phrase betrays narrower scope.
  • C. eTP is community-based prescription transmission; not what CPOE describes.
  • D. CPOE is the term, not the regulatory status. Distractor.
  • E. Network architecture is not what the EP/CPOE/eTP terminology distinguishes.
💡 Examiner's tip

When a vendor or clinician hedges with composite labels ("EP-equivalent", "EHR-ready", "CDS-capable"), the precise functional description is almost always smaller than the label suggests.

MCQ / 2Components

Which of the following is most accurately described as the integration layer of an EP system?

A
The medicines database holding structured drug data with route, formulation, and dose granularity.
B
The active CDS engine that fires interruptive alerts during prescribing.
C
The set of interfaces and APIs that allow the EP system to exchange data with pharmacy, pathology, EHR, and discharge modules.
D
The administration module that records who gave what medication, when, with barcode support.
E
The audit-trail subsystem that logs every change for governance.
Why C is correct

The integration layer is the set of interfaces and APIs that move data between the EP system and adjacent systems — pharmacy, pathology, EHR, discharge, supply chain. Each of the other options is a distinct component of the EP architecture in its own right.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. Medicines database is the data layer; conceptually different.
  • B. CDS engine is its own component, sitting on top of the data layer.
  • D. Administration module is a workflow component, not an integration mechanism.
  • E. Audit trail is a cross-cutting subsystem, not the integration layer.
💡 Examiner's tip

Architecture questions reward learners who can name and isolate each component (data, UI, CDS, integration, administration, audit). The components are six, not "the EP".

MCQ / 3Evidence

Fowlie et al. (Ayrshire, Scotland) reported significant reductions in inpatient prescribing and medicines-administration errors after EP, but no statistically significant reduction in discharge-prescribing errors. Which is the most plausible explanation?

A
Discharge prescribing was already a more structured task with less baseline variability, so EP had less room to reduce error rates.
B
Discharge prescriptions in Scotland were not eligible for inclusion in the EP system at the time.
C
The Ayrshire study did not have sufficient sample size for any error category.
D
Discharge errors are predominantly administration errors, not prescribing errors.
E
Pharmacist intervention rates were higher at discharge, masking any EP effect.
Why A is correct

The lecture's interpretation, drawn from Fowlie's discussion, is that discharge prescribing is more structured — fewer fields, more tightly templated, less variability in baseline practice — so EP has less room to add error-reduction value. The inpatient and administration phases hold most of the error volume; that is where EP's biggest absolute reductions sit.

Why each other option is wrong
  • B. Discharge prescribing was within scope of the Ayrshire EP system; the inclusion question is not the issue.
  • C. Sample size was sufficient for inpatient and admin to reach significance, so the explanation cannot be a generic power problem.
  • D. Discharge errors are prescribing errors at the discharge transition; not administration errors.
  • E. Pharmacist intervention is roughly comparable across phases; not a documented confounder in this study.
💡 Examiner's tip

"Context matters" is the single most reusable phrase in EP-evidence essays. EP impact is heterogeneous across settings and phases, and that heterogeneity has design and clinical reasons.

MCQ / 4CDS design

In the high-usability / high-safety 2 × 2 matrix discussed in the lecture, which scenario is the most dangerous and most common failure pattern in real implementations?

A
High safety, high usability — the design ideal that most systems aspire to.
B
High safety, low usability — clinicians develop workarounds that erode the safety benefit.
C
Low safety, high usability — clinicians use the system fluently but the system permits unsafe orders silently.
D
Low safety, low usability — the system is abandoned and reverted to paper.
E
All four quadrants are equally common in practice; no single quadrant dominates.
Why B is correct

The most insidious quadrant is high safety, low usability: the system is built with extensive alerts, mandatory fields, and confirmations, but the burden on clinicians is intolerable. They develop workarounds — batch scanning, dummy entries, paper-then-input, alert dismissal habits — and the safety benefit erodes invisibly. The Koppel BCMA workaround literature is the canonical example. The system reads as safe on paper but is unsafe in practice.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. The ideal quadrant; not a failure pattern.
  • C. A real failure pattern (a CPOE without CDS), but generally less dangerous than B because the absence of safety controls is at least visible to a careful clinician.
  • D. The system is rejected before damage is done; the project fails openly rather than the patient covertly.
  • E. Empirically not true; the workaround quadrant dominates the literature.
💡 Examiner's tip

"Safety achieved by design but eroded by use" is the single most exam-worthy paradox in health informatics. Recognise it whenever workarounds appear in the stem.

MCQ / 5Workflow

Which of the following is the strongest design control against omission errors in EP prescribing?

A
A free-text comment field that allows the prescriber to clarify any item the structured form did not cover.
B
Required structured fields for route, frequency, and duration, with the order ineligible for signature until complete.
C
A pop-up at signing that asks "Have you completed all required fields?".
D
An audit report run weekly that lists prescriptions with missing data.
E
Pharmacist verification at the time of dispensing.
Why B is correct

Burton-on-Trent and Wirral implementations approached zero omission rates by enforcing required structured fields at the point of order signature. This is upstream control at the most effective layer of the workflow. Each other option either depends on the prescriber being already careful (A, C), or catches the error downstream after potential harm (D, E).

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. Free text reintroduces Magrabi-style discrepancies.
  • C. A self-attestation prompt becomes click-through behaviour within days.
  • D. Weekly audit is too downstream and post-hoc.
  • E. Pharmacist verification is essential defence-in-depth, but it is not a design control on the prescribing event itself.
💡 Examiner's tip

For "strongest control" questions, position the answer at the most upstream point in the workflow that prevents the error from being created.

MCQ / 6Paediatrics

Jani et al. reported a fall in prescribing-error rate from 77.4% to 4.8% in a paediatric renal cohort after EP implementation. Which error subtype accounted for most of this improvement?

A
Drug-drug interaction errors caught by active CDS.
B
Omission errors — items missing from the prescription that EP forced to be specified.
C
Allergy-mismatch errors prevented by the EHR cross-check.
D
Wrong-patient errors prevented by BCMA at administration.
E
Therapeutic-duplication errors caught by class-level alerts.
Why B is correct

Jani et al. attributed most of the improvement to omission errors — paper paediatric prescriptions frequently missed weight, route, or frequency, and EP forced these to be specified. The 77.4% baseline rate sounds extraordinary in adult terms, but reflects that paediatric prescriptions on paper routinely had multiple defects per chart, most counted as omissions.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. DDI errors are not the dominant subtype in a renal-paediatric cohort whose error profile is dominated by dose adjustment, frequency, and weight.
  • C. Allergy errors are a real EP benefit but a small fraction of overall paediatric prescribing errors.
  • D. BCMA addresses administration, not prescribing.
  • E. Duplication is not the dominant error in a renal-paediatric cohort focused on dose individualisation.
💡 Examiner's tip

Attribute paediatric improvements to omission control + weight-based dose calculation. Memorise the Jani 77.4% → 4.8% number and the Warwick 8.8% → 4.6% number for the discussion section of any EP essay.

MCQ / 7BCMA limits

Which of the following is a structural limitation of BCMA that BCMA itself cannot resolve?

A
Nurses occasionally scan medications in batch at the start of shift rather than at the bedside.
B
Wireless coverage in older wards is inconsistent and creates scanning black-spots.
C
Compounded preparations and ward-prepared injectables do not carry manufacturer barcodes and require hospital-applied labels.
D
Agency staff do not know the scanning workflow on their first shift.
E
Nurses press the override button when a scan fails on the first attempt.
Why C is correct

Items A, B, D, and E are all solvable workflow / infrastructure / culture problems within BCMA's scope. C is a structural limitation: BCMA cannot scan a barcode that does not exist on a compounded or repackaged product. The hospital must add a label — a process step BCMA is not designed for and cannot enforce. Original-packs requirement is the lecture's named structural constraint.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. Workaround culture, addressable by training, audit, and design.
  • B. Infrastructure problem, addressable by wireless investment.
  • D. Awareness problem, addressable by induction and just-in-time training.
  • E. Behaviour problem, addressable by display of compliance metrics and design of override logic.
💡 Examiner's tip

"Structural limitation" means a problem outside BCMA's design space. The question is testing whether you can separate structure from operations.

MCQ / 8CDS

Seidling et al. reported that an in-house high-dose CDS algorithm fired on only 4.5% of prescriptions, and that clinicians overrode 75% of those alerts. Which is the most defensible interpretation?

A
The algorithm was too aggressive and was generating unnecessary alerts.
B
The algorithm fired rarely (suggesting good specificity) but the high override rate suggests poor positive predictive value at the alert level — clinicians often had a defensible reason to continue.
C
75% override means 75% of patients received an unsafe dose.
D
The algorithm was successfully preventing 4.5% of prescribing errors.
E
In-house CDS rules are always inferior to vendor-supplied rules.
Why B is correct

The 4.5% firing rate suggests the rule was reasonably specific (it didn't fire on routine prescriptions). But the 75% override rate means that, when it did fire, three-quarters of clinicians decided the alert was wrong — implying the alert's positive predictive value for an actual high-dose error was around 25%. This is the diagnostic-test framing of CDS performance: rare firing + high override = good specificity, weak PPV. Either the rule needs refinement, or the cases that genuinely require dose flexibility (renal/hepatic adjustment, paediatrics, oncology) need to be carved out.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. If the algorithm were too aggressive, the firing rate would be higher than 4.5%.
  • C. Override does not equal unsafe; it equals "clinician disagreed with the alert in this context".
  • D. Cannot conclude prevention from firing alone; firing without harm is not prevention.
  • E. An overgeneralisation; the lecture explicitly notes both in-house and vendor approaches have trade-offs.
💡 Examiner's tip

Treat any CDS metric like a diagnostic test: sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV. Override rate is informative only paired with firing rate.

MCQ / 9Workflow

A nurse on a busy ward reports she cannot administer a regular dose of paracetamol to her patient at 14:00 because the EP system has a 4-hour PRN lockout active and the patient already received PRN paracetamol at 12:00. Which is the correct interpretation of this design behaviour?

A
A design fault — regular orders should never be blocked by PRN lockouts; the lockout is checking the wrong scope.
B
Correct system behaviour — paracetamol total dose includes PRN doses for the 4-hour minimum interval and 24-hour ceiling check.
C
A network failure mimicking a lockout — the nurse should reset the connection and retry.
D
A pharmacy intervention — the pharmacist has on-hold the regular paracetamol pending review.
E
Alert fatigue — the lockout is firing falsely and should be overridden.
Why B is correct

A correctly designed paracetamol dose check operates on total cumulative dose, regardless of whether the dose was prescribed regular or PRN. The minimum-interval rule (≥ 4 h between doses) and the 24-hour ceiling (≤ 4 g/day) apply to the patient, not the order type. So at 14:00, two hours after a 12:00 PRN dose, the lockout is correctly preventing a fifth-hour timing violation. The nurse should defer the 14:00 dose to 16:00 or contact the prescriber to review the PRN.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. The opposite is true — checking total dose across order types is good design, not bad design.
  • C. A network failure would not present as a coherent "4-hour lockout" message.
  • D. An on-hold is a different state and would surface different messaging.
  • E. Cannot assume false alert without checking the cumulative dose first; this is exactly the alert that should not be overridden.
💡 Examiner's tip

PRN-and-regular dose-checking is a classic design exam question. The correct system always reasons over the patient, not the order line.

MCQ / 10Standards

Which standard provides the clinical-terminology spine that allows allergies, problems, and medications to be linked to one another in an EP system?

A
HL7 v2.4 messaging.
B
FHIR R4 RESTful API.
C
SNOMED CT.
D
ICD-10-CM.
E
DICOM.
Why C is correct

SNOMED CT is the international clinical terminology spine that lets allergies, problems, procedures, and medicines be encoded with semantic relationships ("substance X is a beta-lactam", "diagnosis Y is a contraindication for substance Z"). dm+d and RxNorm sit on top of SNOMED for medicines specifically.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. HL7 v2 is messaging — the envelope, not the content semantics.
  • B. FHIR is a transport & resource API; it carries SNOMED-coded data, but is not itself the terminology.
  • D. ICD-10-CM is a classification for billing and epidemiology, not the relational spine for clinical reasoning.
  • E. DICOM is for medical imaging, unrelated to medication terminology.
💡 Examiner's tip

HL7/FHIR = transport. SNOMED = terminology. dm+d/RxNorm = medicines dictionary. LOINC = lab observations. ICD-10 = classification. Memorise this five-line table.

MCQ / 11Governance

Six months after EP go-live, prescribing-error reports rise by 15% above the paper baseline. The board is alarmed. What is the best initial response?

A
Suspend EP and revert to paper until the cause is identified.
B
Conduct a structured root-cause review that disentangles enhanced detection from true error increases, identifies design and training contributors, and reports back on a defined timeline.
C
Switch off the decision-support module to reduce alert burden.
D
Run a system-wide retraining session for all prescribers.
E
Replace the EP vendor.
Why B is correct

The lecture's case-study framing makes this a textbook answer. An apparent rise in errors after EP go-live can be artefact (better detection), real (design or training problems), or a mix. The board's first action is a structured RCA with a defined scope and timeline, not a knee-jerk reversal. This is the J-curve in operational form.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. Reverting to paper would re-introduce illegibility, transcription, and lost-chart errors. Disproportionate.
  • C. Switching off CDS removes a primary safety mechanism; alert fatigue is fixed by tuning, not by deletion.
  • D. Premature; cannot prescribe training without diagnosis.
  • E. Vendor change is a years-long, high-disruption decision; not an initial response.
💡 Examiner's tip

For governance scenarios, the highest-scoring answer is almost always "structured analysis first, intervention proportionate to findings."

MCQ / 12Jordan context

In the Jordanian healthcare landscape, which of the following best describes the current EP integration boundary?

A
Hakeem provides a fully integrated single-record system across MoH, RMS, KHCC, JUH, KAUH, all private hospitals, and all community pharmacies.
B
Hakeem covers MoH, RMS, KHCC and a growing set of integrated facilities, while a substantial private-sector tail (private hospitals, most community pharmacies) operates on independent systems with limited interoperability — boundary errors concentrate at this seam.
C
Jordan operates exclusively on paper-based prescribing and has not yet adopted EP at any level.
D
JFDA mandates EP for all dispensing and has integrated all community pharmacies into a single eTP backbone.
E
Jordan uses the UK NHS Spine directly as its national EHR backbone.
Why B is correct

This is the accurate sociotechnical picture: Hakeem (Electronic Health Solutions, est. 2009) has progressively integrated MoH facilities, the Royal Medical Services, KHCC, and a growing list of teaching hospitals, but the private hospital sector and the community-pharmacy network are largely outside this footprint. Boundary errors — including the kind described in Case 2 — concentrate where data crosses between integrated and non-integrated systems. Recognising this seam is the foundation of any pragmatic EP-strategy answer.

Why each other option is wrong
  • A. Overstates Hakeem's footprint substantially.
  • C. Factually incorrect — Hakeem is in active operation at major facilities.
  • D. JFDA is the regulator; it does not operate community-pharmacy eTP infrastructure.
  • E. No such relationship exists.
💡 Examiner's tip

For Jordan context questions, anchor on (1) the integration footprint, (2) the unintegrated tail, (3) the seam where boundary errors concentrate. This frame works for KHCC, JUH, KAUH, RMS, and private-sector questions alike.

MIX / 1True / False

Statement: a CPOE system without integrated decision support has been shown to reduce overall medication errors by approximately the same magnitude as a full EP system.

TRUE
FALSE
Why FALSE is correct

CPOE without CDS reduces transcription and legibility errors but does not reduce dose-related, allergy-related, interaction-related, or duplication errors at the same magnitude. The Bates and subsequent literature shows that most of the error reduction in EP comes from CDS, not from data capture alone. Comparing CPOE-only to full EP without distinguishing CDS presence is a meta-analytic trap.

Whenever a stem says "approximately the same magnitude" across two interventions, it is usually conflating heterogeneous systems. Examine the CDS layer.

Which is the most defensible governance metric for an EP CDS program?

A
Total number of alerts fired per month.

MIX / 4MCQ

Which of the following is the strongest argument against deploying a hard stop on all paediatric high-dose prescriptions?

A
Hard stops are technically infeasible in modern EP architectures.
B
A hard stop transforms a soft alert into an absolute block, which can delay urgently needed treatment in cases where the high-dose flag is a false positive (renal indications, oncology, sepsis dosing).
C
Paediatric prescribing should never be subject to electronic constraints.
D
Hard stops violate Ministry of Health regulations.
E
Hard stops require additional licensing fees from the EP vendor.
Why B is correct

This is the Strom argument applied to paediatrics. Hard stops are appropriate for absolute contraindications (intrathecal vincristine, daily methotrexate); they are inappropriate for situations where clinical judgment legitimately exceeds default ceilings (oncology dose intensification, sepsis loading doses, renal-replacement therapy). Blanket hard stops on paediatric high-dose prescriptions over-trigger and delay urgent care.

The Strom warfarin/co-trimoxazole study is the lemma you should cite whenever the question asks about hard stops outside of absolute never-events.

MIX / 5MCQ

A pharmacist proposes routing the override-justification text from every overridden alert into a free-text field that is then sampled monthly for governance review. Which is the most accurate critique of this proposal?

A
Free-text override justifications generate Magrabi-style discrepancies and unstructured data that are slow and error-prone to review at scale; the proposal would be improved by a structured-reason picker plus optional free-text comment.
B
Override-justification capture is illegal in most jurisdictions.
C
Override audits do not improve safety under any circumstances.
D
Sampling is statistically unsound; only 100% review is acceptable.
E
Monthly is too infrequent — daily review is the only acceptable cadence.
Why A is correct

A is the precise sociotechnical critique: free text is review-hostile at scale and reintroduces Magrabi-class data-quality problems. A structured reason-code picker (e.g., "patient already on this drug", "renal-adjusted dose, intentional", "drug stopped before alert read") makes monthly governance review feasible. Free-text comments can remain optional for the rare unstructured situation.

💡 Examiner's tip

"Structured reason picker + optional free text" is the canonical compromise between data quality and clinical nuance.

The five sociotechnical lessons in one page

  1. EP ≠ plug-and-play. Implementation is a continuous program, not a project with an end date.
  2. Design determines benefit. The interface, the workflow, the alert configuration, and the data structure decide whether the system reduces or creates errors.
  3. Culture matters. Engagement, ownership, and just-culture incident reporting decide whether the system is embraced or worked around.
  4. Integrate, don't isolate. EP is one component in a sociotechnical fabric — EHR, pharmacy, pathology, discharge — and only delivers when the fabric is woven together.
  5. Monitor and adapt. Post-implementation surveillance is the safety mechanism that catches the J-curve, the workarounds, the unintended consequences. No system is "done" the day it goes live.

For your exam

  • Memorise the eight study-finding pairs (Bates, Koppel, Fowlie, Poon, Strom, Huntemann, Magrabi, Jani).
  • Recite the standards stack (HL7/FHIR, SNOMED CT, dm+d/RxNorm, LOINC, GS1, ICD-10) and which layer each occupies.
  • Reach for the safety / usability 2 × 2 matrix whenever a question describes workarounds.
  • Prefer "most upstream design control" over downstream catches when an MCQ asks for the strongest intervention.
  • For Jordan questions: name the integration footprint (Hakeem at MoH/RMS/KHCC), the unintegrated tail (private hospitals, most community pharmacies), and the seam between them.

Common exam traps to avoid

  • Confusing CPOE with EP — CPOE is data capture only.
  • Treating override rate as a stand-alone metric without firing-rate denominator.
  • Recommending hard stops outside of absolute never-events.
  • Reading paper-revert proposals as a defensible response to a J-curve increase.
  • Treating Hakeem as if it integrated the entire Jordanian health sector.
